\section{5G Networks}

Until \ac{5g}, each successive generation of mobile networks delivered incremental gains in throughput and reach while preserving the same fundamental paradigm: a purpose-built, hardware-centric network optimized for a single dominant use case. \ac{5g} breaks from this pattern. Rather than simply extending data rates, 5G was designed from the ground up as a programmable, multi-service platform capable of simultaneously serving heterogeneous vertical industries with vastly different performance requirements.

To address these diverse requirements, the ITU-R IMT-2020 framework defined three canonical usage scenarios: \ac{embb}, targeting peak data rates in the tens of gigabits per second; \ac{urllc}, targeting sub-millisecond latency and near-perfect reliability for mission-critical applications; and \ac{mmtc}, supporting connection densities of up to one million devices per square kilometer for \ac{iot} deployments \cite{IMTFrameworkoverallobjectivesfuturedevelopment2015}. The 5G system achieves this flexibility through the adoption of key enabling technologies such as \ac{nfv}, \ac{sdn}, Network Slicing, and \ac{mec}, that transform the network into a software-defined, service-based, distributed infrastructure.

The most relevant network functions of the \ac{5g} core are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \ac{upf} is responsible for user plane packet handling such as routing/forwarding, marking and \ac{qos} enforcing;
    \item The \ac{amf} manages registration, connection, mobility management and authorization and authentication;
    \item The \ac{smf} manages the establishment, modification, and termination of data sessions by configuring the \ac{upf};
    \item The \ac{pcf} defines and enforces rules for \ac{qos}, charging and access control;
    \item The \ac{nef} exposes the capabilities and events of the \ac{5g} core to external applications and third parties in a controlled, authorized way;
    \item The \ac{nrf} allows network functions to register and discover each other, acting a central service registry and discovery point for the Service Based Architecture;
    \item The \ac{udm} stores and manages subscriber data and handles authentication credential generation and access authorization;
    \item The \ac{ausf} performs the actual authentication of the \acp{ue} using the credentials from the \ac{udm};
    \item The \ac{nssf} determines which network slice instances should serve the \ac{ue} based on the set \acp{sla} based on subscription info, policies, and requested slice types;
    \item The \ac{af} represents external or operator applications which are able to interact with the \ac{5g} core via the \ac{nef}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{5G-Advanced and the 6G Vision}

In the latest years, the \ac{3gpp} standardization roadmap extended the 5G baseline through a series of incremental releases, collectively referred to as 5G-Advanced \cite{3GPPDigitalcellulartelecommunicationssystemPhase}. These releases introduce native \ac{ai} and \ac{ml} capabilities across both the \ac{ran} and core network, enabling data-driven optimization of resource management, handover decisions, and load balancing. Further enhancements address cross-domain network slicing, extended support for \ac{xr} and immersive services, reduced-capability device classes targeting \ac{iiot} and wearables, and significant improvements in energy efficiency. The trajectory established by these releases directly anticipates the requirements being formalized for the sixth generation under the ITU-R IMT-2030 framework.

% 6g usage scenarios 
The ITU-R defines six usage scenarios for IMT-2030, expanding upon the three scenarios introduced in IMT-2020 while introducing new scenarios that previous generations were not designed to support \cite{IMTFrameworkoverallobjectivesfuturedevelopment2023}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Immersive Communication}: Extends \ac{embb} to cover high-bandwidth, low-latency interactive experiences, including \ac{xr}, holographic telepresence, and multi-sensory communications;
    \item \emph{Hyper Reliable and Low-Latency Communication}: Extends \ac{urllc} to address mission-critical use cases where failure to meet reliability and latency     requirements carries severe consequences, such as industrial automation, emergency services, and telemedicine;
    \item \emph{Massive Communication}: Extends \ac{mmtc} to support the connection of a massive number of devices and sensors across domains such as smart cities, logistics, agriculture, and environmental monitoring.
    \item \emph{Ubiquitous Connectivity}: Addresses the digital divide by extending meaningful coverage to rural, remote, and underserved areas through interworking with non-terrestrial networks and other access systems.
    \item \emph{Artificial Intelligence and Communication}: Integrates distributed \ac{ai} workloads, including model training, inference, and computing resource 
    orchestration, natively into the network infrastructure, enabling use cases such as autonomous driving assistance and digital twin creation;
    \item \emph{Integrated Sensing and Communication}: Extends the radio interface beyond connectivity to provide sensing capabilities, including positioning, object detection, environmental monitoring, and real-time mapping, without requiring dedicated sensor hardware.
\end{itemize}

\section{Network Functions Virtualization}

% intro and driving factors
Traditional network architectures rely heavily on proprietary hardware appliances for each specific function, such as firewalls, routers, and load balancers. These hardware-based solutions are not only costly but also inflexible, making it challenging to scale and adapt to dynamic network demands. 

\ac{nfv} allowed the transition from hardware-dependent network functions to virtualized software solutions. By leveraging virtualization techniques, \ac{nfv} allows for dynamic deployment of various network functions on \ac{cots} hardware as \acp{vnf}. This approach decouples the functions of the network from proprietary hardware, enabling them to run on standard servers, storage, and networking equipment.

As stated in \cite{ETSINetworkFunctionsVirtualisationNFVArchitectural2014}, \ac{nfv} brings several benefits over traditional approaches:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Hardware Independence}: \acp{vnf} can be deployed on any \ac{cots} hardware, reducing the dependency on proprietary hardware; 
    \item \emph{Flexibility and Scalability}: Network functions benefit from increased flexibility and scalability, as they can be deployed and scaled dynamically and distributed across multiple locations; 
    \item \emph{Capital Efficiency}: \ac{nfv} reduces capital expenditures by enabling the use of \ac{cots} hardware and reducing the need for specialized hardware;
    \item \emph{Operational Efficiency}: \ac{nfv} simplifies the management and operation of network functions, reducing the operational expenditure;
    \item \emph{Rapid Service Innovation}: \ac{nfv} enables rapid deployment of new services and features, reducing the time-to-market;
    \item \emph{Vendor Interoperability}: By leveraging standardized and open interfaces, \ac{nfv} promotes a multivendor ecosystem where \acp{vnf} and infrastructure components from different providers can coexist and interoperate.
\end{itemize}

Figure \ref{fig:nfv_architecture} presents the high-level architecture of the \ac{nfv} framework, as detailed by \ac{etsi}. The architecture is composed of three main domains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{\acp{vnf}}: Software implementations of network functions capable of running on \ac{nfvi} resources;
    \item \emph{\ac{nfvi}}: The hardware and software resources that host \acp{vnf}, including compute, storage, and networking resources;
    \item \emph{\ac{mano}}: The management and orchestration system responsible for managing the lifecycle of \acp{vnf} and \ac{nfvi} resources.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{NFV Reference Architecture}

Figure \ref{fig:nfv_reference_architecture} presents the \ac{nfv} reference architecture, as defined by \ac{etsi}, providing a more comprehensive view of the \ac{nfv} architecture. The \ac{nfv}-\ac{mano} domain is further divided into three building blocks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{\acp{vnfm}}: responsible for managing the lifecycle of \acp{vnf}, including instantiation, scaling, updating, and termination. It interacts with both the \ac{vim} and \ac{nfvo} to ensure the \ac{vnf} operates as intended within the \ac{nfv} infrastructure.
    \item \emph{\acp{vim}}: manages the \ac{nfv} infrastructure resources, such as compute, storage, and networking, within a single administrative domain. It provides resource abstraction and allocation, enabling the \ac{nfvo} and \ac{vnfm} to deploy and manage \acp{vnf}.
    \item \emph{\acp{nfvo}}: is responsible for orchestrating and managing network functions across multiple \ac{vim} instances and ensuring the overall coordination of resources and services within the \ac{nfv} environment. It also handles policy-based resource optimization and \ac{sla} management.
\end{itemize}

The other components of the framework are the \ac{oss}/\ac{bss}, which allows for \ac{nfv} service providers to interact with the \ac{nfv} infrastructure, enabling service provisioning, billing, and customer management; the Service, \ac{vnf} and Infrastructure Descriptions, which contain templates/descriptors used internally within the \ac{nfvo} to be translated into functional blocks; and the \ac{ems} which manages the functionality of one or more \acp{vnf}.

\section{Network Slicing}

The heterogeneous nature of 5G use cases imposes a fundamental tension on network design: the performance requirements of \ac{embb}, \ac{urllc}, and \ac{mmtc} services are not only distinct but often irreconcilable within a single, monolithic network configuration. A configuration tuned for peak throughput necessarily compromises the deterministic latency and reliability demanded by mission-critical applications, while one hardened for ultra-reliability wastes resources when serving bandwidth-intensive media services. Network slicing resolves this tension by enabling the concurrent operation of multiple logically isolated, independently configured network instances over a shared physical infrastructure, each optimized for the characteristics of its target service.

A network slice is an end-to-end logical network instance comprising a set of network functions and associated resources (compute, storage, and transport) configured and isolated to satisfy the specific \acp{kpi} of a given service or customer vertical. Each slice spans the full network stack, from the \ac{ran} through the transport domain to the 5G core, and is defined by a \ac{nst}, which captures the performance targets, resource budgets, and topology constraints that govern instantiation and operation. Slices are isolated from one another across the data, control, and management planes, ensuring that faults and congestion within one slice cannot propagate to others.

Network slicing introduces several capabilities over a monolithic network design \cite{3GPP5GSystemarchitecture5GSystem2026}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Logical Isolation}: Each slice operates as an independent network instance, ensuring that traffic, faults, and resource contention within one slice do not affect others;
    \item \emph{Customized \acp{qos}}: Slice-specific configurations enable delivery of differentiated service profiles tailored to each vertical, from high-bandwidth media delivery to ultra-reliable industrial control;
    \item \emph{Multi-tenancy}: Multiple operators, enterprises, or service providers can share the same physical infrastructure while receiving logically dedicated resources and isolated management planes;
    \item \emph{Rapid Service Deployment}: Slices can be instantiated, modified, and decommissioned programmatically through standardized interfaces, reducing the time-to-market for new services;
    \item \emph{Efficient Resource Utilization}: Resources can be dynamically allocated and reallocated across slices according to demand, improving overall infrastructure efficiency compared to statically partitioned, purpose-built networks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Network Slicing Architecture}

The management of network slices in the \ac{3gpp} architecture, as defined in TS 23.501 \cite{3GPP5GSystemarchitecture5GSystem2026}, is organized into three hierarchical functional layers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{\ac{csmf}}: translates high-level customer-facing service requirements into network slice requirements, acting as the interface between business intent and the network management plane;
    \item \emph{\ac{nsmf}}: manages the lifecycle of \acp{nsi}, coordinating the instantiation and operation of constituent sub-slices and enforcing end-to-end \acp{sla};
    \item \emph{\ac{nssmf}}: manages the lifecycle of individual \acp{nssi} within \ac{ran}, transport and core, allocating and configuring the specific resources required by each sub-slice.
\end{itemize}

Network slices are identified through the \ac{nssai}, a collection of one or more \acp{snssai}. Each \ac{snssai} comprises two fields: the \ac{sst}, which encodes the service category, and an optional \ac{sd}, which allows operators to differentiate among multiple slices of the same service type. The \ac{3gpp} specification standardizes four \ac{sst} values: \ac{embb} (1), \ac{urllc} (2), \ac{mmtc} (3), and \ac{v2x} (4). The 24-bit \ac{sd} field provides an operator-defined namespace for further differentiation.

Slice selection is handled at registration time. The \ac{ue} includes a requested \ac{nssai} in its registration request, which the \ac{amf} forwards to the \ac{nssf}. The \ac{nssf} evaluates the request against the \ac{ue}'s subscription profile, operator policy, and current resource availability, returning an allowed \ac{nssai} and, where necessary, a redirection to an \ac{amf} set associated with the target \ac{nsi}.

An end-to-end \ac{nsi} is composed of multiple \acp{nssi}, one per network domain - \ac{ran}, transport and core. The \ac{ran} sub-slice configures the radio resources and \ac{ran} functions assigned to the slice; the transport sub-slice establishes the connectivity between the \ac{ran} and the core; the core sub-slice instantiates the relevant 5G core network functions with the appropriate configurations and policies. Sub-slices may be shared across multiple \acp{nsi} where isolation requirements permit, enabling more efficient use of underlying resources.

The lifecycle of a network slice spans five phases: preparation, in which the \ac{nst} is onboarded and feasibility is verified; instantiation, in which the constituent \acp{nssi} are created and configured; activation, in which the slice begins carrying live traffic; operation, in which the slice is continuously monitored and dynamically adapted to changing demand; and decommissioning, in which the slice is gracefully terminated and its resources released. Lifecycle management is coordinated between the \ac{nsmf} and the domain-specific \ac{nssmf} instances, following the interface specifications defined in 3GPP TS 28.530 \cite{3GPP5GProcedures5GSystem5GS2026}.

% mec host
The \ac{meh} comprises the \ac{mep} and the \ac{vi} that provides the computing, storage and networking resources necessary for the deployment and operation of \ac{mec} applications. The virtualization infrastructure includes a data plane that facilitates the execution of traffic rules and ensures seamless routing among applications, services, and networks.

% mec platform
The \ac{mep} is designed to enable the discovery, advertisement, and consumption of \ac{mec} services by applications. It performs several key functions, such as managing traffic rules, configuring \ac{dns} services, and hosting \ac{mec}-specific services.

% mec applications
\ac{mec} applications operate as virtualized entities (e.g., containers or virtual machines) within the \ac{mec} host. They rely on the \ac{mep} to access essential services, register their requirements and interact with other applications or external systems. The \ac{mep} validates and manages the application requirements, ensuring compliance with resource constraints and operator policies.

% reference points for host
The functionalities described above are supported by well-defined reference points. The Mp1 reference point establishes a connection between the \ac{mep} and \ac{mec} applications, enabling service registration, discovery, and communication. Through Mp1, \ac{mec} applications can also activate traffic rules and facilitate session state relocation when required. Mp2 connects the \ac{mep} to the \ac{vi}'s data plane. This connection allows the \ac{mep} to control how traffic is routed among applications, networks, and other services. The Mp3 reference point facilitates communication between different \ac{mep} platforms. 

% mec management
The management of these components is split between two primary levels: host-level management and system-level management. Host-level management is responsible for managing the individual \ac{mec} host resources, while system-level management is responsible for managing the \ac{mec} system as a whole.

% mec orchestrator
The \ac{meo} is responsible for the system-level management of the \ac{mec} system, overseeing deployed hosts, available resources, and accessible services. It handles application \ac{lcm}, such as onboarding, instantiation, and termination of \ac{mec} applications. It also manages application relocation across hosts when required, ensuring optimal service delivery and resource utilization.

% mec platform manager and vi manager
The host-level management is handled by the \ac{mepm} and the \ac{vim}. The \ac{mepm} oversees the lifecycle of applications, handling tasks such as rule enforcement, conflict resolution, and \ac{dns} configuration. It ensures seamless integration and operation of applications with the \ac{mep}. The \ac{vim} allocates, monitors, and releases the virtualized resources required for hosting \ac{mec} applications. It also reports performance metrics and fault conditions to the \ac{mepm}, facilitating efficient resource management.

% reference points for management
The Mm1 reference point connects the \ac{meo} to the operator's \ac{oss}, facilitating application instantiation and termination requests. Mm2 links the \ac{oss} to the \ac{mepm} for platform configuration, fault management, and performance monitoring. Additionally, the Mm3 reference point enables direct communication between the \ac{meo} and the \ac{mepm}, streamlining application \ac{lcm} and service coordination. The Mm4 reference point links the \ac{meo} to the \ac{vim}, ensuring effective allocation and tracking of virtualized resources. Mm5, on the other hand, connects the \ac{mepm} with the \ac{mep}, supporting configuration, lifecycle support, and application relocation procedures. Other reference points, such as Mm6 and Mm7, further extend the interaction between \ac{mepm}, \ac{vim} and \ac{vi} to manage resources and optimize application deployment.

% oss
The \ac{oss} is responsible for managing the \ac{mec} system's operational and business aspects, including service provisioning, billing, and customer management. It interacts with the \ac{meo} and \ac{mepm} to facilitate service instantiation, configuration, and monitoring, and receives requests from the \ac{cfs} via the Mm8 reference point for service delivery.
